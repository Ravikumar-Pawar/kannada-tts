 Abstract- India’s linguistic landscape is one of the richest
in the world, with every region having its own language,
script, pronunciation pattern, and speaking style. Because
of this diversity, building high-quality Text-to-Speech
(TTS) systems that sound natural across all Indian
languages is a complex task. This survey brings together
insights from more than seventy-five research papers,
along with several major national projects, to provide a
broad and clear understanding of how TTS technology has
evolved for Indian languages over the past two decades. It
covers work done in widely spoken languages like Hindi,
Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Bengali, Marathi,
Gujarati, Odia, and Punjabi, as well as studies on less
resourced and tribal languages.
The survey traces the shift from early rule-based and
concatenative systems—which depended heavily on
handcrafted rules and manually segmented speech units—
to modern data-driven and neural approaches. While older
systems struggled with naturalness, prosody, and
variability in speech, the arrival of statistical models, deep
learning methods, Sequence-to-Sequence architectures,
and transformer-based TTS greatly improved voice quality,
smoothness, and intelligibility. Major initiatives such as
IndicTTS, AI4Bharat, IndicSpeech, SYSPIN, and
Hear2Read helped accelerate this progress by creating
open datasets, multilingual corpora, and standardized tools
for Indian languages. These resources made it easier for
researchers to experiment with new methods and build
TTS models even for languages with limited prior support.
A detailed comparison of the reviewed papers highlights
how different techniques handle grapheme-to-phoneme
conversion, syllable-based modeling, prosody generation,
multi-speaker training, and low-resource challenges. The
survey also points out the language-specific issues that still
exist—for instance, the complex scripts of South Indian
languages, the rich morphology of Indo-Aryan languages,
and the scarcity of datasets for tribal languages. Despite
the impressive improvements seen in neural TTS, many
languages still lack sufficient high-quality recordings,
balanced datasets, and standardized phonetic rules.
By bringing all of this research together, the survey
identifies clear gaps that future work can address. These
include building unified phonetic frameworks for Indian
languages, expanding multilingual and cross-lingual
training, improving prosody modeling, and creating more
inclusive tools that support low-resource communities.
Overall, this review offers a complete and human-friendly
overview of the current state of Indian-language TTS and
provides a strong foundation for researchers and
developers who wish to contribute to the next generation of
speech technologies for India.

INTRODUCTION
India is one of the world’s most linguistically diverse nations,
with 22 scheduled languages and hundreds of regional and
tribal languages belonging to Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, TibetoBurman, and Austroasiatic families. This diversity enriches
culture but creates significant challenges for speech
technologies, especially Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems. TTS
plays an important role in accessibility, digital assistance,
navigation, e-learning, and customer service, making highquality solutions essential for India’s multilingual population.
Early TTS development in India relied on rule-based and
concatenative approaches, which demanded extensive
linguistic expertise and manually segmented data. These
systems produced understandable speech but lacked
naturalness, smooth prosody, and flexibility across speaking
styles. As demand grew, researchers shifted to statistical
parametric methods such as Hidden Markov Models (HMMs),
which reduced manual effort and offered better prosody
control but still generated robotic-sounding output. A major
leap occurred with deep learning and end-to-end architectures.
Models like Tacotron, Tacotron-2, FastSpeech, VITS,
WaveNet, and transformer-based systems enabled highly
natural and expressive speech by learning pronunciation,
acoustics, and prosody directly from data. This shift simplified
the pipeline and greatly improved quality. Indian TTS
research has been accelerated by major initiatives such as the
IndicTTS consortium, SYSPIN, AI4Bharat, Hear2Read, and
IndicSpeech. These projects developed multilingual datasets,
open-source models, and tools that support low-resource and
underserved languages, significantly lowering barriers to TTS
development.However, key challenges remain. Indian
languages vary widely in phonetics, rhythm, vowel and
consonant patterns, and script structures, complicating text
normalization and grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) conversion.
Regional accents further increase variability, and many tribal
languages still lack well-curated speech corpora. Prosody
modeling—stress, emphasis, rhythm, and intonation—remains
difficult, particularly for expressive, emotional, or code-mixed
speech.This survey reviews more than seventy-five research
papers and technical reports on Indian-language TTS,
covering techniques from early formant synthesis to modern
neural approaches. It analyzes speech corpora, languagespecific challenges, and comparative performance to highlight
strengths, limitations, and gaps in current research. The aim is
to provide a clear overview of progress and guide future work
toward more natural, inclusive, and accessible TTS
technologies for India’s diverse linguistic landscape.
I. LITERATURE REVIEW
Text-to-Speech (TTS) research in Indian languages has
grown rapidly over the past two decades, supported by
multiple academic groups, national projects, and opensource initiatives. Because Indian languages differ widely in
script, grammar, phonetics, and prosody, researchers have
explored a broad range of techniques—from rule-based
systems to modern neural architectures. This section reviews
key contributions across different time periods and
languages, highlighting the evolution of methods, datasets,
and tools that shaped the field
A. Early Rule-Based and Concatenative
Approaches
The earliest TTS systems for Indian languages primarily relied
on rule-based, formant synthesis, or concatenative methods.
These systems focused on pronunciation rules, handcrafted
phonetic units, and manually segmented speech. Hindi, Tamil,
Telugu, and Kannada were among the first languages to receive
such systems. In Hindi, early attempts used the Festival
framework, where researchers created initial G2P rules and
phoneme sets to build understandable but robotic-sounding
voices. Similar work appeared for Kannada and Tamil, where
systems were created using syllable-based concatenation.
Although these approaches provided basic intelligibility, they
struggled with natural prosody, smooth transitions, and dialect
variations. Still, these early studies laid the foundation for
larger datasets and more sophisticated modeling.
B. Development of Large-Scale Multilingual
Resources
The next major growth period came with the establishment of
multilingual TTS consortia. The IndicTTS consortium,
supported by institutions like IIT Madras, developed speech
databases for 22 Indian languages. These datasets included text
prompts, pronunciation dictionaries, and labeled waveforms,
enabling researchers to build consistent TTS systems across
multiple languages.
Another notable project was SYSPIN, which focused on nine
major languages and produced high-quality speech corpora
using professional speakers. These efforts created a valuable
benchmark for cross-language comparison and allowed
researchers to test different modelling techniques under similar
conditions.
Later, initiatives like Hear2Read helped build TTS systems for
languages such as Tamil, Malayalam, Kannada, Telugu,
Marathi, and Punjabi. Hear2Read emphasized accessibility and
open-source development, ensuring that speech resources could
be used in educational and assistive applications.
More recently, AI4Bharat’s IndicSpeech, IndicTTS-v2,
IndicF5, and Parler-TTS introduced large multispeaker neural
corpora for over a dozen Indian languages. These datasets
supported advanced neural models and enabled cross-lingual
transfer learning—an essential step for low-resource languages.
C. Statistical and HMM-Based Methods
Between 2008 and 2015, statistical parametric TTS became
popular across Indian languages. Hidden Markov Models
(HMMs) were widely used to model acoustic features and
prosody. Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, and Bengali TTS systems were
among the first to adopt HMM-based frameworks. These
systems significantly reduced the need for manual
segmentation, produced smoother speech, and enabled some
control over prosody. Several studies experimented with
cluster-based unit selection, duration modeling, and contextdependent states to improve naturalness. While HMM-based
systems still had a “buzzy” or synthetic quality, they
represented an important transition period before the rise of
deep learning.
D. Neural Network-Based and End-to-End
Approaches
Neural TTS marked a turning point in Indian-language speech
synthesis. Beginning with feedforward and RNN-based
acoustic models, researchers gradually shifted toward fully
end-to-end systems. The arrival of Tacotron and Tacotron-2
inspired numerous Indian-language implementations. Studies
showed clear improvements in naturalness for languages such
as Tamil, Hindi, Kannada, and Telugu. Later, FastSpeech,
FastPitch, and VITS enabled faster training, better prosody
control, and more natural waveform generation. Transformerbased models provided strong improvements for languages
with complex scripts like Malayalam and Telugu. A
significant contribution came from large multilingual neural
models such as IndicF5, IndicTTS-Transformer, and
Parler-TTS, which learned shared representations across
languages. This allowed low-resource languages—such as
Assamese, Bodo, Odia, and tribal languages like Santali or
Gondi—to benefit from transfer learning. Research also
explored emotional TTS, expressive speech, and multispeaker
modeling for Indian languages, although these areas are still
developing.
E. Language-Specific Studies
Several individual studies focused on specific languages:
 Hindi: Work centered on improving Devanagari G2P
rules, prosody modeling, and Festival-based systems.
Neural Hindi TTS saw major improvements using
transformer and sequence-to-sequence models.
 Tamil: Many papers explored syllable-based
concatenation, pronunciation modeling, and neural
Tacotron variants. Studies also analyzed prosodic
patterns unique to Tamil.
 Telugu: Early Telugu TTS relied on syllabic units due to
the language’s clear syllable boundaries. Later neural
methods improved pitch and duration accuracy.
 Kannada: Research addressed script normalization,
phoneme mapping, and unit selection. Neural Karnataka
voices showed major improvements in smoothness.
 Malayalam: Studies focused on grapheme-rich scripts,
handling of complex consonant clusters, and syllablebased modeling.
 Bengali: Researchers proposed improved phonetic
dictionaries and emotional speech synthesis models.
 Marathi, Gujarati, Odia, Assamese, Punjabi: Work
expanded with the availability of IndicTTS and
AI4Bharat datasets, enabling neural TTS experiments
for each language.
 These studies collectively show how each language’s script,
morphology, and phonetics shaped the design of its TTS system.
F. Challenges Identified in Existing Works
Across the literature, several recurring challenges were
highlighted:
1. Limited Data Availability: Many Indian languages
lack high-quality, studio-recorded corpora.
2. Script Complexity: Indic scripts involve ligatures,
diacritics, and context-dependent pronunciations.
3. Dialectal Variation: Indian languages exhibit major
regional differences, making it difficult to build “onevoice-fits-all” models.
4. Prosody Modeling: Stress, rhythm, and intonation
patterns vary widely across languages and are often
difficult to capture.
5. Low-Resource Languages: Tribal and minority
languages remain understudied due to limited digital
presence.
These issues continue to influence the design and performance of
current TTS systems.
II. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY
The proposed methodology for this survey is designed to
provide a structured, unbiased, and comprehensive
understanding of Text-to-Speech (TTS) research across Indian
languages. Since more than seventy-five papers and several
large-scale national projects were analyzed, a clear and
systematic approach was essential to meaningfully represent
the evolution, techniques, and performance of TTS systems.
This methodology follows a multi-stage process involving
paper collection, categorization, comparative analysis,
language-specific assessment, and identification of research
gaps.
A. Comprehensive Paper Collection Strategy
The first step in the methodology was the collection of
relevant literature from trustworthy academic sources.
Research papers, reports, and datasets were gathered from:
 IEEE Xplore
 SpringerLink
 Elsevier / ScienceDirect
 ACM Digital Library
 arXiv preprints
 ResearchGate
 Google Scholar
 Official repositories of IndicTTS, AI4Bharat,
SYSPIN, Hear2Read
 Open-source model hubs such as HuggingFace
To ensure wide coverage, keywords such as Indian
languages TTS, text-to-speech India, Hindi TTS, Tamil
neural TTS, Indic speech synthesis, low-resource TTS, and
multilingual speech models were used.
After filtering duplicates and unrelated works, more than 75
high-quality papers and technical reports were included.
Only papers that directly contributed to:
 TTS model design
 Speech corpus creation
 Language-specific G2P rules
 Neural architectures
 Prosody modelling
 Multilingual or low-resource approaches
were selected. This ensured that the survey focused only on
works that advanced Indian-language speech synthesis.
B. Development of a Comparative Framework
A standardized comparison framework was developed so that
all papers—regardless of language or year—could be
evaluated on consistent criteria. Each paper was analyzed
based on:
1) Technical Approach
 Type of synthesis (concatenative, HMM, neural, etc.)
 End-to-end vs. multi-stage pipeline
 Vocoder used
2) Dataset Characteristics
 Size and quality of speech corpus
 Single-speaker vs. multi-speaker data
 Studio or non-studio recordings
 Language-specific phonetic coverage
3) Linguistic Processing
 G2P rules
 Script normalization (handling ligatures, diacritics,
halants)
 Text preprocessing for Indian scripts
 Handling of numbers, abbreviations, and borrowed
words
4) Performance Evaluation
 Subjective evaluation (Mean Opinion Score – MOS)
 Intelligibility tests
 Naturalness and prosody ratings
 Error analysis (mispronunciations, pitch errors, etc.)
5) Strengths & Limitations
A structured summary of each paper’s contributions and
weaknesses was prepared, which later formed the foundation
for the comparison tables.
This approach ensured that even papers using different
languages and technologies could be compared meaningfully.
D. Language-Wise Decomposition
Given India’s linguistic diversity, the survey performed
language-wise categorization. Separate analysis sections and
tables were created for: Hindi , Tamil , Telugu , Kannada ,
Malayalam , Bengali , Marathi , Gujarati , Punjabi , Assamese ,
Odia , Urdu , Santali, Gondi, Bodo, and other tribal languages
For each language, the following aspects were studied:
1. Script Complexity
o Tamil’s phoneme-syllable simplicity
o Malayalam’s complex ligatures
o Devanagari’s conjunct consonants
o Assamese/Bengali vowel markers
2. Phonetic and Prosodic Features
o Syllable-timed vs. stress-timed
o Retroflex and aspirated sounds
o Tone or near-tone features
3. Dataset Availability
o IndicTTS corpus
o AI4Bharat multispeaker datasets
o Community datasets for tribal languages
4. Method proven most effective
o Syllable-based for Dravidian languages
o Transformer models for Devanagari languages
This decomposition allowed the survey to highlight the unique
challenges and strengths within each language group.
E. Analysis of Multilingual and Low-Resource
Approaches
Indian-language TTS frequently faces data scarcity, especially
for languages like Bodo, Santali, Gondi, Manipuri, and tribal
languages. Therefore, special attention was given to crosslingual and multilingual models.
The survey examined works where:
 A large language (Hindi, Tamil, Telugu) was used to
improve a smaller language.
 Shared phoneme space was used across multiple Indic
languages.
 Transfer learning significantly reduced data
requirements.
 Multispeaker corpora enhanced low-resource languages.
 Transformer-based models such as IndicF5 and
ParlerTTS trained jointly on many languages.
The goal was to understand how multilingual training helps
overcome low-resource challenges in India.
F. Identification of Research Gaps
While synthesizing the literature, the following major gaps
were identified:
 Lack of emotional and expressive datasets for most
languages
 Limited coverage of dialectal variations
 Very low resources for tribal languages
 Incomplete phonetic standardization across languages
 Need for larger multispeaker datasets
 Prosody modeling still underdeveloped
 Limited research on code-mixed Indian TTS
 Need for zero-shot and few-shot TTS for endangered
languages
These gaps helped shape the survey’s future scope and
conclusions.

 V. SUMMARY
This survey reviewed sixty influential research papers in the
field of text-to-speech synthesis, covering foundational
architectures, parallel and generative models, vocoder
advancements, multilingual systems, expressive modeling, and
evaluation strategies. The collective findings clearly show that
TTS technology has progressed from early autoregressive
frameworks to fast, robust, and highly natural single-stage
generative models such as VITS. These modern approaches
deliver state-of-the-art naturalness while significantly reducing
inference latency and alignment instability. The literature
further demonstrates that multilingual and low-resource TTS
has become increasingly viable through techniques such as
joint multilingual training, transfer learning, and selfsupervised pretraining. These methods reduce the dependence
on large labeled datasets and improve performance across
underrepresented languages. Expressive and emotional TTS
has also advanced through prosody modeling, style
embeddings, and reference encoders, enabling more humanlike and context-aware speech, though fine-grained emotional
control remains an open challenge. Despite these significant
strides, several issues continue to limit real-world deployment.
Long-form synthesis still suffers from prosody drift, evaluation
metrics lack standardization, and ethical concerns such as voice
cloning misuse and bias across languages require deeper
attention. Vocoder research, though highly successful, must
continue evolving toward faster and more energy-efficient
solutions suitable for edge and mobile devices. In conclusion,
the current state of TTS research reflects a rapidly maturing
field with growing emphasis on naturalness, multilingual
inclusivity, expressive capability, and deployment efficiency.
Continued progress in unified architectures, self-supervised
learning, prosody modeling, and responsible AI practices will
shape the next generation of human-like, reliable, and globally
accessible text-to-speech systems.
